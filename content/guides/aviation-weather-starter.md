Weather is not a box to check before a flight. It is part of every decision a pilot makes: whether to go, when to leave, what route is reasonable, and when the safest plan is to wait.

### Build the habit before you can fly

Choose one nearby airport and look at its weather each day for a week. You do not need to decode every abbreviation immediately. Notice the basics first:

1. **Wind** — direction and speed; compare it with the runway layout.
2. **Visibility and clouds** — what can a pilot actually see, and is the sky changing?
3. **Temperature and dew point** — how close are they, and what does that suggest about moisture?
4. **Forecast trend** — is the weather improving, holding steady, or becoming less predictable?

Keep a small notebook. Write what you saw, then look out the window or compare it with a local radar image. The goal is to connect coded information with the sky—not to make a go/no-go decision on your own.

### What to study next

The FAA's [Aviation Weather Handbook](https://www.faa.gov/regulationspolicies/handbooksmanuals/aviation/faa-h-8083-28b-aviation-weather-handbook) explains observations, forecasts, advisories, and aviation weather tools. Its [Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge](https://www.faa.gov/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/phak) also includes weather theory and services.

For actual flight planning, use current official sources and learn the process with a CFI. Weather can change quickly, and this guide is for study—not an operational briefing.
